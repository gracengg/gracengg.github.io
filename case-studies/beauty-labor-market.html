Beauty and the Labor Market

Does the labor market reward above-average appearance, penalize below-average appearance, or both? This study uses robust wage regressions and formal comparisons to separate the central finding from variation across wage quantiles and gender.

Author
Grace Ngo · Dickinson College
Sample
1,260 workers
Historical Wooldridge beauty dataset
Methods
OLS, robust inference, conditional quantile regression and interactions

Read the full research paper PDF · 21 pages ↗

The research question

An average wage gap can combine differences in education, experience, jobs and other worker characteristics. I replicated a wage equation associated with Hamermesh and Biddle (1994), then examined whether the appearance association changes with the control set, across conditional wage quantiles or between men and women.

The analysis uses a public historical teaching extract with interviewer-rated appearance. Average-rated workers provide the reference category. The research focuses on conditional wage associations in that sample.

The central finding

12.4% lower fitted wages

The full-model below-average appearance contrast is −12.4% on the transformed log-wage scale, with a 95% confidence interval from −19.0% to −5.4%.

A smaller estimated premium

The above-average appearance contrast is +1.2%, with a 95% confidence interval from −4.7% to +7.5%. The positive premium is imprecisely estimated.

Asymmetry tested directly

A formal test rejects equal-and-opposite penalty and premium magnitudes at the 5% level (p = 0.032), supporting the asymmetry in the fitted contrasts.

How I tested the result

  1. Keep the comparison consistent. Estimate three nested OLS specifications on the same 1,260 observations, adding worker, job and location controls in stages.
  2. Report economically interpretable estimates. Transform log coefficients and interval endpoints using 100 × [exp(b) − 1], with HC1 robust inference.
  3. Test distributional differences. Estimate five conditional quantiles and use 499 paired bootstrap replications to compare the 90th- and 10th-percentile coefficients directly.
  4. Specify gender comparisons fully. Include the female main effect and both appearance interactions, then calculate within-gender contrasts using their full covariance.
  5. Check sensitivity. Compare HC1 and HC3 inference, omit the service-industry control and exclude the highest 1% of wages as a separate sensitivity exercise.

What the extensions establish

QuestionFinding reported in the paper
Does the gap differ across conditional wage quantiles?Point estimates are more negative at the 90th percentile, but the direct 90th-versus-10th comparison is not statistically significant (p = 0.225).
Is the below-average appearance gap larger for women?The fitted within-gender gaps are both about −12.3%. The gender interactions are jointly insignificant (p = 0.318).
Is the main coefficient sensitive to the reported checks?The below-average log-wage coefficient ranges from −0.129 to −0.140 across the reported sensitivity specifications, close to the full-model estimate of −0.133.

Why this research matters

The paper demonstrates the ability to replicate a result, extend the research question and explain which comparisons answer it. Its strongest conclusion is a negative adjusted association for below-average appearance in the historical sample. The design estimates associations rather than a causal effect or a contemporary hiring trend.

The full paper includes the model specifications, three research figures, regression tables, sensitivity checks, references and numerical appendices.
